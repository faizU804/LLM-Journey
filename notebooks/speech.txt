Here is a speech of about 500 words, built on what I found about this year’s events on both sides of the Line of Control. 
It makes the case that critics and protesters make, and I’ve added the other perspectives at the end.

Speech: Promises, Power, and the People of Kashmir

Brothers and sisters, friends,

Tonight I ask one question: why do those in power keep playing games with the people of Kashmir?

Look at this year. In Azad Kashmir, a civil movement of traders, lawyers, students and transporters asked for
 governance reform and an end to the twelve assembly seats reserved for refugees. They were not given answers. 
 On June 5, the authorities declared the Joint Awami Action Committee a proscribed organisation under the region’s 
 anti-terrorism law. 
 Around 72 people were detained within eighteen hours. Sedition cases were ordered against four of its leaders. 
 The protests that followed turned 
 violent and left about 30 people dead.

Thirty families lost someone. When people ask for rights and are answered with bans, arrests and bullets, that is not governance. It is a game in which the people always lose.

And when the protesters were blamed, the story shifted again. The federal information minister said the movement had links to India 
and wanted to weaken Kashmir’s relationship with Pakistan. Is every hungry, frustrated citizen a conspirator? Or is it easier to blame
 outsiders than to answer your own people?

Now look across the Line of Control. The same pattern of promises made and promises delayed is there too. Years ago, the Union
 Home Minister said delimitation would come, then elections, then the restoration of statehood. Delimitation came. Elections came. 
 Statehood did not. On September 28, the Jammu and Kashmir Assembly passed a resolution calling for the immediate restoration of full statehood.
  The Chief Minister reminded the House that the people had been told the sequence of steps. A promise made to millions cannot be treated like an item on a calendar.

So I say to every government on every side: Kashmiris are not a card in a diplomatic game. We are not a slogan for election rallies.
 We are not a line in a speech about patriotism.

We have watched leaders speak of Kashmir with love and then treat Kashmiris with suspicion. We have seen committees formed, 
talks started and talks abandoned, all while ordinary life goes on in fear, and young people leave because they see no future here.

What do we ask for? Not much. We ask to be heard before we are banned. We ask that a peaceful protest not be called terrorism.
 We ask that a promise made in parliament be kept. We ask that our elected voices be respected and that our dead be counted honestly.

A government that is confident in its cause does not fear its own people. It sits down with them. It listens, it negotiates, and it keeps its word.

To those in power, I say: the people of Kashmir have long memories. They remember every promise. And they will keep asking, 
peacefully and persistently, until the games end and the answers begin.

Thank you.

Other perspectives: Governments in this dispute would answer differently. Azad Kashmir’s prime minister said he had invited 
the protesters to resume talks and that elections would not be postponed. Islamabad says the JAAC’s activities threaten public order. 
In India, the central government’s position is that statehood will come through the constitutional process and that security conditions and timing matter.
 Views also differ sharply on how many people have died and who was responsible, so you may want to soften or cut specific numbers if you give this speech.
